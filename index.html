Five tools, one sensor

Everything the meter reads

Every tab pulls from the same lux reading — the meter just asks a different question of it.

Exposure tab

Exposure

Aim the sensor at the projected image and press measure. The reading converts straight to seconds, scaled against Zone System zones III through VIII. An f-stop transform slider handles push and pull adjustments; a filter grade transform compensates the instant you change contrast filters.

Contrast tab

Contrast

Spot-meter the negative's highlight and shadow. The meter recommends a single contrast grade and one exposure time that satisfies both ends — and shows the Delta EV, the negative's actual contrast range in stops, so you know before you print whether a straight grade will hold it.

Split grade tab

Split Grade

When a negative's range exceeds what one grade can hold, the meter switches to two exposures — a soft-filter pass and a hard-filter pass — and gives you the percentage split and exact time for each.

Virtual proof tab

Virtual Proof

Scan the easel in a grid, up to 40 × 30 cells, and watch the meter paint a grayscale preview of the print before a single sheet of paper is exposed. A histogram and per-cell zone, density, and clipping detail come with it. Auto-scan mode waits for each reading to stabilize before it moves on.

Filter & paper editor

Filter & Paper Editor

Manufacturer filter factors are lab averages — your bulb, your lens coatings, and your reflector will drift from them by 10–30%. The editor lets you enter your own ISO R values per filter brand, set your paper's base speed, and estimate a calibration for a paper you haven't measured directly yet.

Before your first print

Fifteen minutes, once per paper

The meter needs to learn your paper's speed before it can give you a time. It's a single test print — do it once per emulsion, and every reading after that is instant.

  1. Pick the paper. Choose its brand and type in the meter.

  2. Set up the enlarger exactly as you'll print — lens stop, head height, sharp focus.

  3. Pull every contrast filter out of the light path. This step matters.

  4. Load a flat, mid-toned negative — a gray card or plain wall works well — and measure the lux at the easel.

  5. Expose a test strip in steps and develop it normally.

  6. Find the segment whose midtone looks exactly right, and note its exposure time.

  7. Enter that lux reading and time into the meter's calibration field.

  8. Measure again — the suggested time should now match what you just proved on paper.

Optional · 5 minutes Run Sensor Offset Calibration in total darkness, then Sensor Light Calibration under moderate light, so readings track your specific unit.
